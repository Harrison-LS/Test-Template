Flights to New Zealand: Plan the Journey, Not Just the Fare

New Zealand flight purchases deserve consideration beyond finding the cheapest option. Many flights involve multiple stops, are long, and define the first several days of the trip. Passengers choose where to fly to, how well they rest, whether they can safely get to a vehicle, and how conveniently they can get to their destination. Advice to “buy the cheapest flight” refers more to a general overview of the situation and less to a solution. A lower cost flight is appropriate, but only if that flight itinerary is not unreasonable. A flight itinerary that saves money but includes an exhausting stopover, a difficult arrival, a tricky way to get to a destination, and unpleasant and lengthy layovers is costly. Most of the time the best solutions for flight itineraries to New Zealand are those that reduce the overall length and the accompanying stress of the trip. Rather than looking for the cheapest flights, look to create the most efficient itinerary. New Zealand is best enjoyed with intentional itineraries and is a poor destination to rush through. A return flight itinerary that goes from one end of the country to the other is most likely more efficient. The savings may not be as financially rewarding as initially thought, especially when domestic travel and accommodations are taken into consideration. Rather than looking for flights immediately, consider the first and last days of your trip. Ask yourself these questions before you begin a flight search:
  • Does the trip really start here?
  • First night is when you arrive in the city (unless you must travel beyond it straight away)?
  • Will the traveller take a train, car, bus, domestic flight or join a tour?
  • Does the route end there (or close)?
  • How much time do you have and is it worthwhile to backtrack the route?

These may also affect the price of a ticket. A return flight may work well for a short break to one region. For a long journey with a direct route, an open jaw ticket might be a better travel choice, even with a higher ticket price. A small price increase may also save an additional day of travel.

Donâ€™t mistake a cheap flight to New Zealand as a good deal. A lot can affect the value of a trip. The timing of the trip along with the number and length of layovers can really affect the trip and how rested the traveler is upon arrival.

When shopping for flights, consider what is included and if there are mandatory additional costs to the ticket price. Also consider if the itinerary is protected if different ticket segments are purchased separately.

A fare that looks very cheap may actually be missing some important amenities that are actually very useful and necessary for a long trip. It is not bad. It just means that the fare needs to be put into context.

There is a difference between a short connection and a reasonable one. The most direct route is not necessarily the route that is the least stressful. There can be perfectly good travel itineraries that have very short connections as long as everything goes to plan. However, a long, convoluted route leaves much more room for delays, changes, and of course, waiting in lines. So the perfect itinerary with just enough padding between connections is definitely worth it. On the other hand, a very long layover can ruin a trip, even if it comes with free travel, meals, and hotel stays.

The right question is not, “What is the lowest price?” It is, “What will this ticket cost in money, time, energy and flexibility?” A flight with a moderate fare, reasonable connection and useful arrival time is definitely the better bargain.

Arrival time is definitely important, and travellers have a bad habit of ignoring it when booking flights to New Zealand. That bad habit means there are a lot of bad things that happen, such as having to wait an hour for transportation just to get a bad, poorly-slept on night. Having a good arrival time to ensure you can have a good arrival day is totally worth it, without a doubt.

An airport area overnight stay will spare some hassle with a late arrival, but can still mean expensive last minute accommodations or transportation.

The first night should be protected. After an international trip, the best course of action is usually the least active. With this in mind, collect your bags, go through customs, and take a short trip to your overnight. Then have something to eat and get some rest. This is not a waste of time. This allows you to rest and get ready for the rest of your trip.

Driving travelers need to be especially careful with their arrival plans. With jet lag and overall fatigue associated with new road systems, it can be a bad decision to save time on a flight and end up with a long and stressful trip and a wasted first day. An overnight stay can actually save time in the long run and make for a more productive trip.

An overnight stay is not always a bad idea, especially if you will be staying in the arrival city for the first night of the trip. As long as you have organized a transfer and a place to stay, a late flight will not break your itinerary. All you need to do is coordinate your arrival with your plans.

Choose stopovers as part of the experience

A stopover is not a traveling necessity. It creates the overall experience of the trip. For New Zealand flights, one good connection is often much better than several bad connections. Many travelers choose the flight with the least amount of overall travel time. This is often a good choice, however it can also result in higher costs because schedules are often rigid. A short connection is not necessarily bad and can often give travelers enough time to eat, charge their phones and devices, stretch, and go to the next gate without too much stress if a delay occurs. Travelers should also keep in mind that time and money spent on comfort can also be time and money well spent. On the opposite side of the argument, if travelers do not arrive at their destination, a long layover is not a vacation. It is time that could have been spent doing something more productive. It is also good to think of how much a long layover would cost if the traveler would have to purchase a hotel and additional meals. Travelers should also understand that a multiple flight itinerary is different than a single flight itinerary. Different flight bookings are often a more cost efficient way to travel or allow more flexible routing, however booking separately also means that travelers take on a greater amount of risk. This may be a greater overall cost than the potential savings. New Zealand has multiple airports, so travelers should be strategic about how they travel and where they fly to. The flight shouldn’t dictate the route, but rather support it. A traveler traversing the North may have differing flight needs than a traveler bound to the South, and a multi-zone trip may need a unique route. It is tempting to choose the itinerary with the lowest fare and figure everything else out later. That is poor planning. The cheapest itinerary may end up being a poor bargain with expensive and time consuming additional legs, with long ground transfers as a poor return at the end of the trip. Conversely, you should not choose a route with large fare differences based on convenience. If one airport is much cheaper and your continuation plans are simple, then that route may be correct. The logical decision is to price the entire first and last day: the flights, the baggage, the local transport, the accommodation (if needed), the domestic connections, the rental car, and the time you would otherwise lose in connecting flights. Only then can you compare the options. If you are taking domestic flights after arrival, it is prudent to have a sufficient window between your arrival on an international flight and your following flight. There are concerns, between arrival and departure, with checked baggage and delays. A stressful start to a great travel itinerary can be avoided by planning for a self-transfer. If the domestic travel is the most important, then a long and comfortable connection or an overnight stop is probably the better option. Flexibility is worthwhile if the trip is important enough. Travelers don't necessarily need the most flexible travel tickets. A low priced ticket with clear rules and restrictions can work great if travel dates are fixed. However, passengers need to assess the costs of flexibility, and if changing that ticket means losing a significant amount of the value or heavily charging them to purchase a new ticket. The trip and its purpose also dictate a flexible ticket. A vacation that is heavily scheduled with accommodations, car rentals, reservations, or connections means a traveler should look into a more flexible ticket as one change with a flight potentially has a downstream effect on other planned travel arrangements. Travelers taking short trips to visit family should also consider a flexible ticket, but if travel plans are unconstrained, then that is less important. Safeguards against unforeseen events can be purchased through travel insurance;

however, this should not be considered a substitute for reviewing fare conditions. Insurance policies have an attached fine print, exclusions, and evidence requirements. The most important protection against unforeseen events is a reasonable booking of flexible travel arrangements. Your first night’s stop should not need the last connection possible. Don’t trust an ‘impossible’ or ‘legal’ airport transfer to get you there. A good flight itinerary allows for normal travel issues. It has easy to access references for booking, timely contact information, all confirmations, and sufficient time for the major travel segments. It should also have a reasonable luggage policy. Assume that key luggage will not be available upon arrival. When booking through an agent, know who is responsible for making changes. If a schedule change results in a lower fare, understand that an intermediary may be introducing an unnecessary layer of complexity. Direct booking is not always the best, especially if an agent provides value and support. Know who has made the booking and how you'll receive support during unforeseen circumstances. The best way to search is to implement a system and be disciplined. This includes broad checking of date ranges and realistic connection airports. Compare different legs of the journey. Once you see cheap alternatives, don't make impulsive decisions. Here's a short list of other things to consider. Total fare after seat reservations, luggage added, booking fees Total travel time, each connection included Connection length and if all segments are protected on the same booking III. Considerations Planning requires several factors to be taken into consideration, which may include: · Time of arrival and feasibility of the first night's plan · Choice of airport in relation to route · Rules and costs for the flexibility of flights, changes, cancellations, and missed connections · Likely costs for onward transport and accommodation and domestic travel It is helpful to prepare two or three full travel itineraries instead of comparing individual flights. One may aim for the lowest total cost. Another may aim for an efficient route with the least amount backtracking. A third may aim for comfort and ease. In most cases, the best option becomes the most obvious choice after the alternatives are presented. Travelers also should avoid over-optimizing. Although it's natural to want to save as much money as possible, it shouldn't be done at the expense of way too much self-inflicted anxiety from searching for the best deal. A flight option that fulfills the basic needs for a reasonable cost and convenient itinerary should probably be booked. The goal shouldn't be to win a fare-searching contest. The goal should be to arrive prepared for a well-planned trip. A strong flight decision supports the entire travel plan. New Zealand flights are more than just a form of transport. They set the pace and the shape of the journey, and the level of resilience required. A low cost flight may be a good deal, but with a suboptimal route and stopovers which cause exhaustion and increase risk, it is not a good deal. Flight decisions transform the entire travel experience from the start to the finish of a trip. It includes what happens upon arrival, and how much energy the traveler is able to expend to enjoy the days of the travel itinerary. This may result in a more expensive flight, but it will definitely be less rushed, and there will be less avoidable expenditures as well as more opportunities for vacation enjoyment. Factors like these have made several other travelers choose the same method over the quickest one offered. In the long run, a less rushed holiday will allow you to experience more of what New Zealand has to offer. Unavoidable costs like eating will make up less of the overall cost and therefore less of the total frustration of your holiday, and you will have less of a need to rush around.
